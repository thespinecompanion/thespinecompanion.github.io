When your spine MRI and your symptoms seem to tell different stories

October 20, 2026 · 3 min read

Turn a confusing scan report into a focused conversation about what fits, what remains uncertain, and what happens next.

In this article
  • Which findings actually fit your symptoms?
  • Describe a day, not just a pain score
  • Ask what another test would change
  • Leave with a short, usable explanation

A spine MRI can leave you with two kinds of uncertainty: a report full of alarming words when your symptoms seem manageable, or a relatively mild report when daily life is very difficult. The useful next step is to have someone connect the scan, your symptoms, and the examination in plain language. You should not have to translate the report into a treatment decision alone.

Before that conversation, separate what the report says from what you are experiencing. Write each on its own half of a page. Leave space between them for the clinician's explanation. This keeps a word such as “degeneration” from becoming your entire story, while still giving an important finding proper attention.

Which findings actually fit your symptoms?

In adults 50 and younger, disc changes on lower-back MRI can appear with or without back pain, though some findings are more common in people with pain. This association does not identify an individual person’s pain source, and it should not be generalized to every age group or every part of the spine.1

For the appointment, underline only the terms you want explained. Ask the clinician to show you the relevant area and explain whether it matches the side, location, and pattern of your symptoms. If the answer is uncertain, ask what information is missing. A useful explanation can include uncertainty without leaving you without a plan.

Describe a day, not just a pain score

Choose two ordinary situations that show the problem clearly. Perhaps sitting through a meal is harder than a short walk, or standing at a counter is difficult while lying down is more manageable. Describe what you notice rather than trying to diagnose why it happens. Include when the pattern started and whether it is changing.

Keep pain, altered sensation, and difficulty doing a task as separate observations. “My foot feels numb” and “I cannot lift my foot the way I could last week” convey different information. If something is new or worsening, contact your care team about the change rather than waiting to perfect your notes or interpret the scan yourself.

Ask what another test would change

When a second scan or a different test is proposed, ask which question it would answer and how the result could change the next step. You can also ask what the plan would be if it did not provide an explanation. This makes the conversation about a decision, rather than collecting more reports to decipher.

Bring previous reports if you have them, and ask the office how to share the actual images before the visit. Confirm that the clinician has received them. If your symptoms have changed since the scan, say so at the start; an old description of how you felt may no longer reflect the question you need answered.

Leave with a short, usable explanation

  • Which finding best fits my symptoms, and which findings may be less relevant?
  • What remains unexplained, and what is our next step for that uncertainty?
  • Which changes should prompt a call, and who should receive it?
  • What can I do within my current care plan while we work this out?

Finish by repeating the explanation in your own words. If all you can repeat is the name of an abnormality, ask for the missing connection to your life. The aim is an understandable plan for your symptoms, including follow-up, rather than a verdict based on how dramatic a report sounds.
