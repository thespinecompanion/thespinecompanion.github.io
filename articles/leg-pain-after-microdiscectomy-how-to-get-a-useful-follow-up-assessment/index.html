Leg pain after microdiscectomy: how to get a useful follow-up assessment

November 11, 2026 · 3 min read

Describe what changed, communicate what daily life looks like now, and ask for a clear plan without guessing whether pain is expected.

In this article
  • Know when a message is not enough
  • Describe the change before the whole history
  • Ask for the reasoning behind the next step
  • Leave with a named next step

Leg pain after microdiscectomy deserves an explanation from your surgical team, especially when it is new, worsening, or disrupting basic activities. Contact the team with a short account of the change and ask how promptly you should be assessed. Comparing your pain with someone else's recovery cannot tell you what is happening in your case.

If you expected immediate relief, it can be difficult to know how to describe a disappointing result without sounding as though you have already decided the operation failed. You do not need to decide that. Your task is to report what you are experiencing; the team's task is to assess it and explain the next step.

Know when a message is not enough

New loss of bladder or bowel control, or new difficulty passing urine, alongside back or leg symptoms needs emergency assessment. These changes can accompany serious spinal nerve dysfunction. Do not wait for a routine portal reply. Follow your discharge instructions for other urgent postoperative changes, and seek urgent medical help if you are unsure whether a severe new change can wait.1,2

Keep the postoperative contact number where you and a helper can find it. Check the after-hours instructions rather than assuming the routine office line is monitored overnight. If you are asking about an urgent change, say that clearly at the beginning of the call instead of leaving it at the end of a long account.

Describe the change before the whole history

Start with the date and name of your operation, then explain whether the leg pain continued without improvement, improved and returned, or appeared somewhere new. Say which leg and which area are involved in plain language. Separate pain from numbness, weakness, or trouble walking if those are also present. Report changes honestly without testing your limits to produce a more precise description.

Include one or two examples of current function: you cannot finish a short necessary walk, sitting for meals is difficult, or sleep is repeatedly interrupted. Mention whether this is different from the instructions or expectations you were given. These details are easier to act on than a long list of every sensation you have noticed since surgery.

  • Operation and date; when the current change started.
  • Where the symptoms are, whether they are worsening, and any new functional difficulty.
  • What the prescribed recovery plan currently involves and what you have actually been able to do.
  • A direct question: how soon do I need assessment, and what should I do while awaiting it?

Ask for the reasoning behind the next step

At follow-up, ask what the examination suggests and what remains uncertain. If a test, treatment change, or period of observation is proposed, ask what question it is intended to answer and when the plan will be reviewed. You can also ask which changes should trigger earlier contact rather than waiting for that review date.

Bring an accurate medication list and note any difficulty following the current instructions. Do not change medicines or restart exercises outside your postoperative plan to see whether that solves the problem. If side effects, cost, transport, or access to rehabilitation are getting in the way, include them in the conversation so that the plan reflects your actual circumstances.

Leave with a named next step

Write down who is coordinating the follow-up, when you should expect contact, and what to do if that contact does not happen. If the explanation remains unclear, ask the clinician to restate what is known and what is still being investigated. You deserve a workable plan even when a definite explanation is not available at the first appointment.
