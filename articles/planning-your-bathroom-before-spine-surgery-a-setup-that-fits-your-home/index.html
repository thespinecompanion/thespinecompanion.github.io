Planning your bathroom before spine surgery: a setup that fits your home

October 26, 2026 · 3 min read

A focused checklist for discussing a shower chair, transfer bench, toilet setup, and practical help before you buy equipment.

In this article
  • Show the team the route, not just the shower
  • Ask for an equipment recommendation you can actually shop for
  • Plan the small tasks around bathing
  • Confirm the plan before you go home

The right bathroom setup after spine surgery depends on both your discharge instructions and your actual room. Before buying a shower chair or transfer bench, ask the rehabilitation or occupational therapy team to review the space and the help you expect to have. The most useful equipment is equipment that fits the plan, the room, and the person using it.

An online photograph cannot show whether a device will work beside your toilet, inside your shower, or with your doorway. You can make the assessment more practical by gathering measurements and photographs in advance. Keep these on your phone or on a single page you can bring to the preoperative appointment.

Show the team the route, not just the shower

Describe how you reach the bathroom from the room where you expect to rest. Include stairs, narrow doorways, thresholds, and the places where you turn. Photograph the shower or tub entrance and the surrounding floor space, with no one using the room. Ask the team which measurements they need before you order anything.

Tell them whether you live alone, whether someone can help at particular times, and whether you have another bathroom available. Be specific about the helper's availability rather than simply saying that family can assist. A plan that relies on morning help needs a different conversation if that person is only present in the evening.

Ask for an equipment recommendation you can actually shop for

If a chair, bench, or toilet aid is recommended, request the relevant dimensions, features, and fitting instructions. Check how the proposed item works with your shower door or curtain and where it will be stored. Ask whether the service can supply it, whether it is covered, and who can help if the delivered model is different from the one discussed.

Avoid improvising a transfer technique from a product video. Ask the therapist to show you the method appropriate to your postoperative restrictions and to include your helper if assistance is part of the plan. If the movement or setup does not feel manageable during teaching, say so before you leave rather than hoping it will become obvious at home.

  • When may I shower, and what are the instructions for my incision or dressing?
  • Which equipment, if any, fits my bathroom and the restrictions planned for me?
  • Who will teach me and my helper how to use it, and when will that happen?
  • What is the alternative if the equipment or home support is not ready at discharge?

Plan the small tasks around bathing

Think through towels, clean clothes, toiletries, and where wet items will go afterward. Ask someone to help arrange supplies where you can access them within your team's instructions. Discuss privacy as well as assistance: you may want help setting up the room without wanting someone present for every part of your routine.

Check delivery times and return rules before ordering bulky items. If you are borrowing equipment, ask the team whether that specific item is appropriate and what needs checking before use. Keep the instructions and supplier contact information together so that a missing part or fitting question does not turn into a last-minute scramble.

Confirm the plan before you go home

Discharge day is a good time to compare the final instructions with what you prepared. If your needs or restrictions have changed, tell the team what is already at home and ask what should change. Leave with a contact for practical problems. A bathroom plan is finished when you know how the whole routine will work, including who will help and what to do if it does not.
