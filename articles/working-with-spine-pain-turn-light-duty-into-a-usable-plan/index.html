Working with spine pain: turn “light duty” into a usable plan

November 6, 2026 · 3 min read

Describe the tasks that are difficult, propose concrete adjustments, and agree how to review what is working.

In this article
  • Translate the job into tasks
  • Ask for a specific change you can evaluate
  • Put the agreement somewhere you can find it
  • Review the arrangement before frustration builds

“Light duty” can sound reassuring until you try to work out what it means on Monday morning. A shift may involve sitting, standing, reaching, carrying, driving, and getting to the workplace before the job even begins. A useful work plan connects your clinician's restrictions to those actual tasks.

Start with the parts of your day that are difficult and the parts you can still manage. You do not need to settle every question about your long-term working life at once. The immediate aim is a clear, practical arrangement with named responsibilities and a point at which it will be reviewed.

Translate the job into tasks

List your main duties using verbs: lifting boxes, answering calls, moving between rooms, serving customers, typing reports. Add details that explain the difficulty, such as the location of supplies or whether a task can be interrupted. Avoid inventing your own weight or movement limits; use the restrictions supplied by your treating professional and ask for clarification where they are vague.

Include the less visible demands. A long commute, an inaccessible restroom, or mandatory meetings without a chance to change position may matter as much as the main job. If your role varies by shift, bring examples from both an ordinary day and a demanding one. A job description alone may miss these differences.

Ask for a specific change you can evaluate

Possible discussion points include moving frequently used supplies, sharing a particular physical task, arranging a different meeting setup, or adjusting how duties are scheduled. These are options to explore with your employer and care team, not assurances that every arrangement is appropriate or available. Focus first on the obstacle you are trying to solve.

For each proposed adjustment, write the intended result. Moving supplies to an accessible location might avoid a task you are currently restricted from doing. A different schedule might make treatment appointments possible. This is more useful than buying equipment on the assumption that an “ergonomic” label will solve every problem.

Put the agreement somewhere you can find it

Clarify who needs the restrictions, who approves changes, and what you should do if an assigned task conflicts with the plan. Ask your clinician to explain any wording you cannot interpret. It is reasonable to request a written summary so you are not trying to reconstruct a conversation while a supervisor is waiting for an answer.

Keep medical information within the appropriate workplace process and decide what you want colleagues to know. You can describe a work need without telling everyone your whole medical history. Local rules and employer processes differ, so use the relevant occupational health, human resources, union, or other support contact for questions about your situation.

Review the arrangement before frustration builds

Set a check-in date and keep brief notes about the work itself: which task remained difficult, which change helped you complete a duty, and where the plan was unclear. This is not a test of whether you are trying hard enough. It is information about whether the arrangement fits the job and your current restrictions.

  • What tasks are allowed, restricted, or still unclear?
  • Who can resolve a conflict between the written plan and a request during a shift?
  • When will the arrangement be reviewed, and what information should you bring?

A workable plan may need revision as your treatment, role, or abilities change. The useful outcome is shared clarity: you know what you are expected to do, your workplace knows what has been agreed, and there is a route for addressing difficulties before they become an improvised negotiation every day.
