Physical therapy for spine pain: what are we trying to change?

October 23, 2026 · 3 min read

A practical way to discuss treatment goals when your scan mentions narrowing, degeneration, or a disc problem.

In this article
  • Separate the scan finding from the therapy goal
  • Make the first plan small enough to understand
  • Agree on what to report between visits
  • Review the plan against your original task

If your scan mentions a narrowed space or a disc problem, being referred for physical therapy can feel puzzling. You may wonder what an exercise appointment is supposed to do about a structural finding. Start by asking the therapist to name the purpose of your particular plan: which activity it addresses, what change you will look for, and when you will review whether it is helping.

That conversation matters more than leaving with a long exercise sheet. Bring the referral, any restrictions your clinician has given you, and one daily task you want to discuss. It might be preparing food at the counter, getting through a work meeting, or walking from the car to an appointment. A specific task gives you something concrete to evaluate together.

Separate the scan finding from the therapy goal

Ask whether the proposed goal is easier movement, a change in symptoms during a task, more confidence with an agreed activity, or something else. If someone says therapy will “open up” or “fix” a space shown on your scan, ask exactly what that means and what evidence would show it happened. Do not assume a phrase describes a permanent change to your anatomy.

Exercise has evidence of benefit for pain in chronic nonspecific low back pain, with smaller average improvements in everyday function. That evidence does not establish that exercise reverses nerve compression, or tell us which program is appropriate after surgery or for a particular nerve problem. Your diagnosis and current restrictions still need to shape the plan.1

Make the first plan small enough to understand

For each home activity, ask for a demonstration, a chance to try it with feedback, and written instructions you can follow. Check the starting amount, how often to do it, and what to do if you cannot manage it. If you have limited space, no suitable floor area, or difficulty getting to appointments, say so before the plan is finalized.

You can request a shorter starting list when several new activities feel unmanageable. Ask which item matters most and which can wait. This is a planning discussion, not permission to alter a postoperative program yourself. If instructions from your surgeon and therapist seem different, ask them to clarify the plan together before choosing between them.

Agree on what to report between visits

Before leaving, ask what response the therapist expects during and after the activities, and what response means you should stop and contact them. Get an explicit answer about new or changing symptoms rather than relying on a general instruction to “push through.” Ask for the best contact route if a problem arises before your next appointment.

Keep your feedback brief and descriptive. Note the activity, what you actually did, what you noticed, and how it affected the rest of the day. “I could not finish preparing dinner afterward” gives the therapist something useful to discuss. There is no need to repeatedly test your limits to produce a more impressive record.

Review the plan against your original task

  • What daily activity are we using to judge progress?
  • What would make us change the amount, the approach, or the goal?
  • When will we review a plan that is not helping?
  • Who should reassess symptoms that do not fit the original referral?

At the review, compare the plan with your original priority, not another person's exercise program. If the activity is still difficult, describe what remains difficult and ask what the next decision is. You should be able to explain why you are doing the work and how the team will decide whether to continue, adjust, or reassess it.
